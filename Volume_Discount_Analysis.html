Executive Summary

I tracked US sales for Toyota, Honda, Nissan, Subaru and Mazda over 35 half-year periods from H1 2008 to H1 2025, alongside CPI, new car finance rates, average loan amounts and GDP growth.

Subaru grew 170.8% from H1 2008 to H1 2025, and its share of this five-brand group rose from 4.8% to 11.8%. Nissan finished 1.1% below where it started and Honda 6.2% below, while Mazda grew 37.3%. In the first half of 2020, Nissan's sales fell 39.9% from a year earlier, the steepest drop of the five. Mazda's fell 7.0%.

Financing rates did not show the negative relationship you'd expect. Four of the five brands sold more on average in high-rate periods than in low-rate ones. Rates and sales both rose over the period, so a simple comparison mixes the rate effect with a long-run trend. Separating the two needs a model with a time trend and price data.

Interpretation

Subaru's 7-point share gain is the clearest story in the data. Every other brand except Mazda lost share of the group. The data doesn't say why. Product mix (Subaru's crossover lineup) and brand loyalty are plausible explanations to test, not conclusions.

022020 hit Nissan hardest

From H1 2019 to H1 2020, sales fell 39.9% at Nissan, 23.8% at Honda, 21.2% at Subaru, 16.8% at Toyota and 7.0% at Mazda. Nissan's H2 2022 sales (320,290) came close to its 2009 recession low (309,296).

What this suggests

Nissan's slump isn't just a 2020 shock. Its sales were already falling after 2017, and by H1 2025 they were still about 30% below H1 2019, while Toyota was above its 2019 level. That's a brand-level question, not a macro one.

03Financing rates don't line up the way you'd expect

I split periods at the median finance rate and compared average sales. Toyota sold 5.4% more in high-rate periods, Mazda 10.6% more and Subaru 25.2% more. Honda was flat (+0.9%) and only Nissan sold less (-2.8%).

Why this isn't a rate effect

Higher rates don't increase demand. Rates were high in 2008, 2018 to 2019 and 2023 to 2025, and sales for most brands also grew over time, so this comparison picks up the long-run trend. A regression with a time trend, GDP and price as controls would separate them.

04A price proxy regression finds no price sensitivity

The closest thing to a price in this dataset is the average new car loan amount. A log-log regression of each brand's sales on loan amount (35 observations per brand) gives the coefficients below.

What this means

Only Subaru is significant, and its coefficient is positive: Subaru sold more as average loans grew. That fits a brand growing while car prices rose over the same years, not a buyer response to price. Loan size also reflects financing choices, not just sticker price, so none of these numbers is a price elasticity.

Robustness check

The Subaru result doesn't survive a second look. Its residuals are strongly autocorrelated (Durbin-Watson 0.16, where 2 means none), so the p-value above overstates confidence. Refitting with a time trend, a half-year dummy and CPI-deflated loan amounts flips every brand's coefficient negative (Subaru: -1.74), and in half-year changes Subaru's is -0.42 (p=0.52). No brand's coefficient keeps the same sign and significance across all three specifications. Loan amounts move almost in lockstep with time (r = 0.96, or 0.88 after adjusting for inflation), so this data can't separate a price effect from long-run growth. The table is in python/Tableau_Data/10_regression_robustness.csv.
